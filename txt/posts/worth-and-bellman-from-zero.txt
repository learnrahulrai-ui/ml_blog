  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The bandits lesson (Part 1) gave one spot and a row of arms. The shopkeeper at his clinic
  counter never moved -- he stayed put, picked which arm to pull, watched the payout arrive,
  and updated his guess of each arm's true average. The worth of an arm was that average.
  Nothing behind it.

  That is the simplest possible case. A real agent lives in a world with many spots, arrows
  between them, and a worth that cannot be pinned to one spot alone: the worth of a spot
  leans on the worth of the next spot, and the next on the one after. The drone in Part 3
  lives in that world -- 27 spots, fixed arrows, worths that circle back on each other.

  Something must be laid before you can grade or improve any plan. That something is what
  "worth" actually means when the world has many spots, when a move can land in more than
  one place by chance, and when the future goes on. This post lays that floor, piece by piece.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A RETURN IS A PILE OF REWARDS, NOTHING MORE

  A reward is a number you grab on a single move. Three moves, collecting +2, then +1, then +3:

    now ──(+2)──► ──(+1)──► ──(+3)──► done
    pile = 2 + 1 + 3 = 6

  That pile -- all rewards grabbed from a moment on, added straight up -- is the return.
  That is all "return" means: an addition. Nothing fancier.

  The worth of a spot is a measure of that pile: "if I stand here and keep moving under some
  plan, how big a pile do I collect?" The next four sections answer what the pile needs before
  you can compute it: what if the rewards are unsure, what if the pile runs forever, does it
  always end, and where do the entries come from?

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  WHEN THE REWARD IS UNSURE, AVERAGE IT

  A reward can be unsure -- the world rolls a die for it on each move. To see what you expect
  per try, run the move many times and read the per-try average.

  One move that pays +1 (chance ½) or +3 (chance ½). Run it 10 times:

    runs:    1   1   1   1   1   3   3   3   3   3      ← five 1s, five 3s
    pile     = 5 × 1  +  5 × 3  =  5  +  15  =  20
    per try  = 20 ÷ 10 = 2

  There is a shortcut that skips the 10 runs -- value × chance, added:

    1 × ½  +  3 × ½  =  0.5  +  1.5  =  2

  Same 2. That per-try number -- value × chance, added -- is the average (the mean).

  The shortcut value × chance, added is the pile-of-10 reasoning applied without the 10 runs.
  If a machine pays random amounts each use, its true average (the mean reward per use) is
  exactly this: each possible payout × its probability, added.

  >> YOUR TURN
     A move pays 0 (chance ¼) or +4 (chance ¾). What is the average reward?

     check your work:   0 × ¼  +  4 × ¾  =  0  +  3  =  3

  Why only the average matters: the worth recipe (coming below) has exactly one slot for the
  reward. Two moves with the same average but different noise drop the same number into that
  slot -- so they produce the same worth. Proof in numbers (landing on a spot worth 10,
  γ = 0.9, which is the fade defined in the next section):

    story A:  pays +2 (chance ½) or 0 (chance ½)  →  average = 1
    story B:  pays +1 flat                         →  average = 1

    story A worth:  1  +  0.9 × 10  =  10.0
    story B worth:  1  +  0.9 × 10  =  10.0

  Same 10. The full noise pattern is irrelevant -- only the mean enters the recipe.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  THE FADE (γ): AN ENDLESS PILE MUST STAY FINITE

  A reward later counts for less than the same reward now. The amount of fading per move is
  one number: γ (gamma). These posts use γ = 0.9.

  A +10 reward. What it is worth to you NOW, depending on when it arrives:

    +10 right now           =  10
    +10, one move off       =  0.9 × 10  =   9.0
    +10, two moves off      =  0.9 ×  9  =   8.1
    +10, three moves off    =  0.9 × 8.1 =   7.29

  Each step further multiplies by 0.9 again. A reward k moves away is worth (0.9)^k × reward.

  The reason for the fade is not impatience -- it is finiteness. Without it, a task that
  runs forever and grabs +1 every move would accumulate:

    1 + 1 + 1 + ...  →  no limit

  That is not a number. With γ = 0.9, the endless +1 pile becomes:

    1  +  0.9  +  0.9²  +  0.9³  +  ...

  Call that whole pile S. Every term after the first is 0.9 times the same pile:

    S      =  1  +  0.9 × S
    S − 0.9 × S  =  1
    S × (1 − 0.9)  =  1
    S × 0.1  =  1
    S  =  1 / 0.1  =  10

  A +1 every move, faded at γ = 0.9, is worth 10 right now. More generally: an endless
  stream of +1 at fade γ is worth 1 / (1 − γ). γ is what keeps an endless pile finite --
  not a mood knob for impatience, but the thing that makes the math work at all.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  EPISODIC TASKS END; CONTINUING TASKS RUN FOREVER

  Two shapes of task:

    EPISODIC  :  start ──► ──► ──► [done]      pile ends when the task ends
    CONTINUING:  start ──► ──► ──► ──► ...     pile grows without stopping

  Episodic: the task hits a terminal spot and stops. The pile is finite on its own --
  no fade needed. The drone from Part 3 lands on the runway: episodic.

  Continuing: no terminal spot. The pile would grow forever. An air-conditioning unit
  that runs and adjusts on and on: continuing. It NEEDS the fade (γ < 1) to make the
  pile a finite number.

  A one-move episodic task (one arrow, then done) has no future. The pile is just the
  reward from that one move. The worth of a bandit arm is exactly this: one-step episodic,
  worth = average reward, no fade. The bandit from Part 1 is the simplest possible case of
  the worth idea -- one spot, one move, nothing after.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  THE WORLD'S DIE: WHERE YOU LAND IS NOT ALWAYS CERTAIN

  When you pick an arrow from a spot, the world rolls a die. That die decides two things at
  once: where you land and what reward you grab. The chances of that roll are written:

    p( landing,  reward  |  spot,  arrow )
       ↑ the outcomes        ↑ what you chose

  Read: "given this spot and this arrow, the chance of landing there with that reward."

  One spot, arrow →, the world's die:

    ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  spot HERE,  arrow →                              │
    │                                                   │
    │  chance ½  →  land on spot X,  reward +2          │
    │  chance ½  →  land on spot Y,  reward +2          │
    │                                                   │
    │  p( X, +2 | HERE, → ) = 0.5                      │
    │  p( Y, +2 | HERE, → ) = 0.5                      │
    └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  This is the only place randomness lives in the whole world model. Every sum you see
  written "Σ over (landing, reward) of p(...) × something" means: average over the die --
  value × chance, added, from the averaging section above.

  In the toy worlds used in Parts 3 and 4, the drone's arrow always lands in exactly one
  next spot (the die has only one outcome). That is a special case of p -- one outcome with
  probability 1.0. The recipe handles both cases; the toy just happens to be the easy one.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A POLICY IS A FIXED MAP OR A DICE OVER ARROWS

  A policy is the rule you use to pick an arrow at each spot. Two kinds:

    ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │  DETERMINISTIC:  one fixed arrow at every spot                │
    │                                                               │
    │    spot S  →  arrow always points left (no dice)             │
    │                                                               │
    │  STOCHASTIC:  a die over the arrows, with chances             │
    │                                                               │
    │    spot S  →  left (chance 0.7),  right (chance 0.3)         │
    └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Deterministic: one fixed arrow per spot. No randomness in the choice.
  Stochastic: you roll to pick your own arrow. The policy itself is noisy.

  Key fact (proved in the "ties" section below): a deterministic optimal policy always
  exists. You are never forced into stochastic arrows to achieve the best possible worth.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  V AND Q: TWO SEPARATE WORTH-MACHINES

  Two machines both hand back a worth. They differ by what you feed in:

    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                                                                 │
    │  V :  spot  ─────────────────────────────►  worth              │
    │        one thing in (where am I?)                              │
    │                                                                 │
    │  Q :  spot + arrow  ────────────────────►  worth               │
    │        two things in (where am I, which move first?)           │
    │                                                                 │
    └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  V( spot ) = worth of being here, then following the map at every spot afterward.
  Q( spot, arrow ) = worth of taking THAT arrow first, then following the map afterward.

  They are not one machine with two inputs -- they are two separate machines. V takes one
  thing; Q takes two. The question "does a value function take 1 or 2 arguments?" has a
  clean answer: there are two value functions, V takes 1 and Q takes 2.

  For a bandit (one spot, one move then done), Q( spot, arm ) = average reward of that arm.
  That is the entire worth of taking the arm. V at the bandit's single spot is the average
  Q over whichever arm the map picks.

  Policy evaluation (Part 3) fills in V at every spot. Policy iteration (Part 4) uses Q
  to choose better arrows. The recipe below defines both.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  THE BELLMAN RECIPE: WORTH = REWARD NOW + FADED WORTH NEXT

  The recipe that defines V and Q for any fixed map:

    Q( spot, arrow )  =  mean reward grabbed now
                       +  γ  ×  average worth of where you land

  "Average worth of where you land" means: for each possible landing spot and reward
  (each outcome of the world's die), take worth-of-landing-spot × chance, then add them.

  Worked. Spot HERE. Arrow →. World's die from the picture above.

  Outcome X (chance 0.5):  land on a spot worth 10,  reward +2
  Outcome Y (chance 0.5):  land on a spot worth  0,  reward +2

  γ = 0.9.

    Q( HERE, → )

      =  chance of X  ×  [ reward from X  +  γ × worth of landing X ]
       + chance of Y  ×  [ reward from Y  +  γ × worth of landing Y ]

      =  0.5  ×  [ 2  +  0.9 × 10 ]
       + 0.5  ×  [ 2  +  0.9 ×  0 ]

      =  0.5  ×  [ 2  +  9.0 ]
       + 0.5  ×  [ 2  +  0.0 ]

      =  0.5  ×  11
       + 0.5  ×   2

      =  5.5  +  1.0

      =  6.5

  >> YOUR TURN
     Same setup. But landing on spot X is now worth 5 (not 10). Landing on Y still worth 0.
     Both outcomes still reward +2 with chance ½. γ = 0.9. Compute Q( HERE, → ).

     check your work:
     outcome X = 0.5 × (2 + 0.9×5) = 0.5 × (2 + 4.5) = 0.5 × 6.5 = 3.25
     outcome Y = 0.5 × (2 + 0.9×0) = 0.5 × 2.0        = 0.5 × 2.0 = 1.00
     Q( HERE, → ) = 3.25 + 1.00 = 4.25

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  GREEDY TAKES THE BEST ARROW -- THE BELLMAN OPTIMALITY EQUATION

  The recipe above grades a fixed map. But what if you want the best possible worth?

  Greedy = at each spot, pick the arrow with the highest Q-value -- the max. The Bellman
  optimality equation replaces "follow the map" with "take the best arrow":

    V*( spot )  =  max over arrows  [ mean reward  +  γ × average V* of next spot ]

  V* is the optimal worth -- the pile you collect under the best possible plan.

  Worked. One spot. Two arrows. Both land on a spot worth 5. γ = 0.9.

    arrow left :  reward = 1  →  1  +  0.9 × 5  =  1  +  4.5  =  5.5
    arrow right:  reward = 2  →  2  +  0.9 × 5  =  2  +  4.5  =  6.5

    V*( spot ) = max( 5.5,  6.5 ) = 6.5    ← right is best

  ** KEY TRAP: the optimality equation holds for V* alone -- NOT for any map that uses
     greedy as its rule. Going greedy on a WRONG worth-map does not make that map obey
     the equation. A map of all-zeros applied greedily just takes the biggest immediate
     reward and has nothing to do with V*. The equation is a statement about V* itself;
     it names no policy. In policy iteration (Part 4), you go greedy with respect to the
     CURRENT worth, not with respect to V* directly -- you iterate until the worth is
     good enough that greedy with respect to it is the same as greedy with respect to V*.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  THE BEST MAP MAY TIE; THE BEST WORTH NEVER DOES

  Sometimes two arrows at a spot are equally good. Draw it:

              spot T
             /       \
          left       right
            ↓           ↓
        worth 5     worth 5     ← equal

  Four facts off this one picture:

    unique best map?                NO   -- "always left" and "always right" both win
    a FIXED (deterministic) best?   YES  -- "always left" has one fixed arrow and is best
    unique best worth?              YES  -- both arrows give 5; spot's best worth is 5
    forced into a stochastic map?   NO   -- "always left" is deterministic and is best

  KEEPER: many best maps can share one best worth. The map may tie; the worth never splits.

  In every finite MDP: a deterministic optimal policy always exists. The optimal worth V* is
  unique -- you might not have a unique best map (when arrows tie), but V* is always one
  number at each spot, not a range.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  ONLY THE MEAN REWARD ENTERS THE RECIPE

  The "only the average matters" fact from the start of this post (two noise stories, same
  average, same worth) has a clean consequence for the recipe. The reward slot holds one
  number -- the mean. Name the mean reward of a spot-and-arrow pair r(s, a). The recipe
  splits cleanly:

    worth( spot ) = r(s, a)       +   γ  ×  Σ  p(landing | spot, arrow) × worth(landing)
                    ↑ mean reward      ↑ faded next-worth, averaged over the die

  The reward slot holds the mean. The future slot holds the average over where you land.
  The split is exact -- nothing is hidden.

  Why name r(s, a) separately? Because in some worlds the landing spot and the reward are
  independent: where you end up does not determine what you earned on the way. The r(s, a)
  split makes that independence visible in the formula.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  γ DECIDES WHICH STRATEGY WINS

  The fade γ does not merely scale the future -- it changes which arrow is best.

  One decision spot. Two arrows:

    left  : grabs +1 now, then keeps earning +1 with no end  →  worth = 1 / (1 − γ)
    right : grabs +2 once, then nothing                      →  worth = 2   (for any γ)

  The worth of "left" uses the geometric-series formula from the fade section above:
  1 / (1 − γ). Turn the γ knob:

    γ = 0.0 :  left = 1 / (1 − 0.0) = 1 / 1.0 = 1.0    right = 2   →  RIGHT wins
    γ = 0.5 :  left = 1 / (1 − 0.5) = 1 / 0.5 = 2.0    right = 2   →  TIE
    γ = 0.9 :  left = 1 / (1 − 0.9) = 1 / 0.1 = 10.0   right = 2   →  LEFT wins

  The winner flips with the fade. Small γ (short-sighted: only now matters) takes the big
  reward now. Large γ (far-sighted: the future matters a lot) takes the small-but-endless
  stream. At γ = 0.5 exactly they tie.

  This means: change γ and you change the best policy. γ is not a detail of the recipe --
  it is a choice that decides what behaviour you are rewarding.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  V IS THE AVERAGE OF Q OVER THE MAP

  V at a spot and Q at a spot-and-arrow are connected by the map:

    V( spot ) = Σ over arrows  [ chance map picks this arrow ] × Q( spot, arrow )

  In words: V is the average of Q over however the map spreads probability across arrows.

  Worked. Q( HERE, → ) = 6.5  (from the Bellman recipe section above).
  Q( HERE, ← ) = 5.5  (given). Map flips a coin: each arrow with chance 0.5.

    V( HERE ) = 0.5 × 6.5  +  0.5 × 5.5
             = 3.25  +  2.75
             = 6.0

  No extra γ. The fade is already inside each Q. Adding it again would double-fade the future
  and give the wrong number.

  The OPTIMAL V uses max instead of the map-weighted average:

    V*( HERE ) = max( Q*( HERE, → ),  Q*( HERE, ← ) )
              = max( 6.5,  5.5 )
              = 6.5

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  A BANDIT IS A ONE-SPOT MDP -- AND Q IS THE ARM'S AVERAGE

  Take an MDP with one spot and one move that ends it (episodic, one step). No next spot
  -- no future -- so an arrow's worth is just its average reward:

    Q( spot, arm ) = average reward of that arm

  That is a bandit: one spot, a row of arms, Q = mean reward per arm. The multi-armed bandit
  from Part 1 is an MDP with one spot and N arrows. Every Q-value there was an arm's average
  payout. The "greedy lock" (first positive estimate captures all future patients) was a
  policy optimization failure in a one-spot MDP. Epsilon-greedy was a stochastic policy.

  V at the bandit's single spot = average of Q over the map's arm-probabilities.

  When two arms are equally optimal (same average), both are best arrows. The equal-average
  condition:

  LEFT pays +1 (chance p) or +3 (chance 1−p). Average of LEFT:

    1 × p  +  3 × (1 − p)  =  p  +  3  −  3p  =  3  −  2p

  RIGHT pays 0 (chance q) or +10 (chance 1−q). Average of RIGHT:

    0 × q  +  10 × (1 − q)  =  10  −  10q

  Set equal -- arms equally optimal:

    3 − 2p  =  10 − 10q
    3 − 2p + 10q  =  10        ← add 10q to both sides
    10q  =  10 − 3 + 2p        ← subtract 3, add 2p to both sides
    10q  =  7 + 2p

  Verify with p = 0.5:  q = (7 + 2 × 0.5) / 10 = (7 + 1.0) / 10 = 8.0 / 10 = 0.8
    average of LEFT   = 3 − 2 × 0.5  = 3 − 1.0  = 2.0
    average of RIGHT  = 10 − 10 × 0.8 = 10 − 8.0 = 2.0   ✓

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  THE FLOOR, COMPLETE

  Every piece above connects to the next:

    a return is a pile of rewards, added
      ↓  but rewards can be unsure
    average: value × chance, added = what you pull per try
      ↓  but an endless pile is not a number
    fade γ < 1  →  endless pile = 1 / (1 − γ), a finite number
      ↓  but does the pile end on its own?
    episodic (task ends, no fade required) vs continuing (runs forever, γ < 1 required)
      ↓  now: where does each pile-entry come from?
    the world's die  p(landing, reward | spot, arrow) -- the only randomness in the model
      ↓  and how do you choose which arrow?
    the map (policy) -- fixed arrow per spot or dice over arrows
      ↓  given a map, what is a spot worth?
    V (one input: spot) and Q (two inputs: spot + arrow) -- two separate worth-machines
      ↓  how do you compute them?
    Bellman recipe: worth = mean reward + γ × average next-worth over the die
      ↓  the recipe grades a fixed map -- what if you want the BEST map?
    Bellman optimality: worth* takes max over arrows (not the map average)
      ↓  is the best map unique?
    the map may tie; a deterministic best always exists; V* itself is unique
      ↓  does γ change which strategy is best?
    yes -- γ flips the winning arrow between now-big and later-endless
      ↓  how do V and Q connect?
    V = average of Q over the map (no extra γ; the fade is already inside Q)

  Policy evaluation (Part 3) takes a fixed map and runs the Bellman recipe at every spot
  until the worths stop moving. Policy iteration (Part 4) also finds the arrows that
  maximize the recipe at every spot. Both assume everything above.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  THE NUMBERS, IN PYTHON

  All code below is hard-coded. No for or while loops. Every intermediate value is
  shown with the actual number, not a variable name standing in for it.

  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  # ── return is a pile ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  r_move1 = 2     # reward on move 1
  r_move2 = 1     # reward on move 2
  r_move3 = 3     # reward on move 3

  pile = r_move1 + r_move2 + r_move3   # 2 + 1 + 3 = 6
  print("return:", pile)                # return: 6


  # ── average of an unsure reward ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  # move pays +1 (chance 1/2) or +3 (chance 1/2)
  reward_low  = 1
  reward_high = 3
  prob_low    = 0.5
  prob_high   = 0.5

  avg_reward = reward_low * prob_low   +   reward_high * prob_high
  #          = 1          * 0.5        +   3           * 0.5
  #          = 0.5                     +   1.5
  #          = 2.0
  print("average reward:", avg_reward)    # average reward: 2.0


  # ── fade γ: +10 faded by distance; endless +1 stays finite ───────────────────────────────

  gamma = 0.9

  worth_now       = 10 * (0.9 ** 0)    # = 10 × 1.000 = 10.0
  worth_1_off     = 10 * (0.9 ** 1)    # = 10 × 0.900 = 9.0
  worth_2_off     = 10 * (0.9 ** 2)    # = 10 × 0.810 = 8.1
  worth_3_off     = 10 * (0.9 ** 3)    # = 10 × 0.729 = 7.29

  print(worth_now, worth_1_off, worth_2_off, worth_3_off)
  # 10.0  9.0  8.1  7.29

  endless_1_per_move = 1 / (1 - gamma)  # = 1 / 0.1 = 10.0
  print("endless +1 at γ=0.9:", endless_1_per_move)   # 10.0


  # ── Q(s,a) from the Bellman recipe ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  # spot HERE. arrow →. two outcomes.
  # outcome X (chance 0.5): land on spot worth 10, reward +2
  # outcome Y (chance 0.5): land on spot worth  0, reward +2

  gamma        = 0.9
  prob_X       = 0.5
  reward_X     = 2
  worth_land_X = 10

  prob_Y       = 0.5
  reward_Y     = 2
  worth_land_Y = 0

  term_X = prob_X  *  (reward_X  +  gamma * worth_land_X)
  #       = 0.5    *  (2         +  0.9   * 10)
  #       = 0.5    *  (2         +  9.0)
  #       = 0.5    *  11
  #       = 5.5

  term_Y = prob_Y  *  (reward_Y  +  gamma * worth_land_Y)
  #       = 0.5    *  (2         +  0.9   * 0)
  #       = 0.5    *  (2         +  0.0)
  #       = 0.5    *  2
  #       = 1.0

  Q_here_right = term_X + term_Y
  #            = 5.5    + 1.0
  #            = 6.5
  print("Q(HERE, →):", Q_here_right)    # Q(HERE, →): 6.5


  # ── V from Q: the map averages the arrow-worths ───────────────────────────────────────────

  Q_right = 6.5   # Q(HERE, →) from above
  Q_left  = 5.5   # Q(HERE, ←) given

  prob_map_right = 0.5   # map flips a coin
  prob_map_left  = 0.5

  V_here = prob_map_right * Q_right  +  prob_map_left * Q_left
  #       = 0.5           * 6.5      +  0.5           * 5.5
  #       = 3.25                     +  2.75
  #       = 6.0
  print("V(HERE):", V_here)          # V(HERE): 6.0

  V_star_here = max(Q_right, Q_left)   # max(6.5, 5.5) = 6.5  ← optimal: take right
  print("V*(HERE):", V_star_here)      # V*(HERE): 6.5


  # ── Bellman optimality: one spot, two arrows, both land on worth-5 spot ───────────────────

  gamma      = 0.9
  worth_next = 5    # both arrows land on a spot worth 5

  Q_left_opt  = 1  +  gamma * worth_next   # = 1 + 0.9×5 = 1 + 4.5 = 5.5
  Q_right_opt = 2  +  gamma * worth_next   # = 2 + 0.9×5 = 2 + 4.5 = 6.5

  V_star = max(Q_left_opt, Q_right_opt)    # max(5.5, 6.5) = 6.5
  print("V*(spot):", V_star)               # V*(spot): 6.5


  # ── γ flips the winner ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  # left : endless +1 stream   worth = 1 / (1 - γ)
  # right: +2 once, then done  worth = 2             (any γ)

  worth_right = 2

  worth_left_at_00  = 1 / (1 - 0.0)   # = 1 / 1.0 = 1.0
  worth_left_at_05  = 1 / (1 - 0.5)   # = 1 / 0.5 = 2.0
  worth_left_at_09  = 1 / (1 - 0.9)   # = 1 / 0.1 = 10.0

  print("γ=0.0 :  left=", worth_left_at_00, " right=", worth_right, "→ right wins")
  print("γ=0.5 :  left=", worth_left_at_05, " right=", worth_right, "→ tie")
  print("γ=0.9 :  left=", worth_left_at_09, " right=", worth_right, "→ left wins")
  # γ=0.0 :  left= 1.0  right= 2 → right wins
  # γ=0.5 :  left= 2.0  right= 2 → tie
  # γ=0.9 :  left= 10.0 right= 2 → left wins


  # ── equal-arms algebra: when do two bandit arms tie? ─────────────────────────────────────

  # LEFT  pays +1 (chance p) or +3 (chance 1-p)  →  average = 3 - 2p
  # RIGHT pays  0 (chance q) or +10 (chance 1-q) →  average = 10 - 10q
  # equal averages  →  3 - 2p = 10 - 10q  →  10q = 7 + 2p

  # verify with p=0.5 → q=(7+2*0.5)/10=0.8:
  p = 0.5
  q = (7  +  2 * p) / 10      # = (7 + 1.0) / 10 = 8.0 / 10 = 0.8

  avg_left  = 3  -  2 * p     # = 3 - 1.0 = 2.0
  avg_right = 10 - 10 * q     # = 10 - 8.0 = 2.0

  print("p=0.5,  q=0.8 :  avg_left =", avg_left, "  avg_right =", avg_right)
  # p=0.5,  q=0.8 :  avg_left = 2.0  avg_right = 2.0   ✓
