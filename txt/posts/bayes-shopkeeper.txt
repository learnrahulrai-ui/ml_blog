  PATH . SPECIAL -- Bayes by Head-Count  (standalone; no other page on this blog needed)

  Every Bayes tutorial gives you the formula:  P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B).  Then it asks
  you to swap the letters around until the answer pops out. That is not an explanation -- it
  is a recipe card with no kitchen. Two things always go missing: WHY the formula inverts the
  question at all, and what happens to the people the machine silently misses.  This page
  builds the kitchen first: four characters in one small story, ten concrete scenarios, and
  every answer arrived at by counting heads in a Sunday market crowd. The formula falls out on
  its own, after you have already used it.

  THE RULE OF THIS PAGE. Assume you remember nothing. Every word is defined where it first
  appears. Anything named more than five lines ago is named again. Every number is worked in
  front of you, never "recall that...". One idea per line. A pencil and this page are the
  whole kit. Decimals shown to four places; rounded to two in summaries.

  TOY NUMBERS, REAL RECIPE. One line of honesty up front. The RECIPE -- the Sunday-crowd
  trick, the two-crowd distinction, the gluing move, Bayes' rule itself -- is exact. The
  NUMBERS are a toy: small crowds (100 or 1,000 souls) and simple machine rates, chosen so
  arithmetic fits on paper. Where a toy choice could mislead, the text says so. Trust the
  recipe; treat the crowd sizes as a sketchpad.

  The one move, named once so you are not surprised later:

      STOP reading fractions as decimals. Make them HEADS IN THE SUNDAY CROWD. Then every
      Bayes question becomes the same plain count: "of the heads holding a BEEP paper,
      how many are truly sick?"

  That single translation turns ten formulas into one arithmetic fact.


  ## The Cast (the people who live inside every probability problem)

  **The market runs on SUNDAY.** We never reason about "the world" in the abstract. We reason
  about the crowd that files past a stall this one Sunday -- 100 souls, or 1,000, or 10,000.
  Turn every fraction into heads in that crowd. That is the whole trick.

  **The greedy shopkeeper** runs a test-stall on market day. Feed him a person, his machine
  prints a paper: BEEP (positive) or silence (negative). He is greedy -- he loves printing
  BEEP papers because each one is a sale. He will NEVER tell you how many BEEP papers he
  prints in total. That hidden number is P(Beep) -- the overall share of the crowd that walks
  out holding a beep. The shopkeeper hides it. We rebuild it from scratch in the next section.

  **The faulty machine** lies two ways:
  - It stays silent on a truly sick person: a MISSED CASE (the textbook calls this a false
    negative, or a miss). The person walks away believing they are fine.
  - It beeps on a healthy person: a GLITCH (false positive). The person walks away alarmed
    for no reason.

  **The doctor** sits downstream. A patient walks in clutching a BEEP paper: "Doctor -- given
  this beep, am I actually sick?" That is the Bayes question. The doctor sees only the beep
  paper; she does not see the crowd behind it.

  **The wild** = outside the stall. Every sick person the machine stayed silent on walks back
  into the wild holding a clean paper -- undiagnosed, untreated, possibly spreading the
  disease, possibly worsening. Bayes arithmetic counts only beepers; it throws the missed
  cases away. That blindness is exactly why missing them is the dangerous part.


  ## The Comma and the Bar (two different crowds answer two different questions)

  Two probability symbols look almost identical but measure completely different crowds.

  **The comma: P(A, B) = JOINT = "both, out of the whole Sunday crowd."**
  P(Sick, Beep) is the share of the WHOLE crowd that is sick AND holds a beep paper. You
  stand outside and count everyone -- healthy, sick, beeping, silent. The denominator is the
  full Sunday crowd.

  **The bar: P(B | A) = CONDITIONAL = "out of one shrunk back-room."**
  P(Beep | Sick) is the share of the beepers ONLY AMONG the sick. First, throw out everyone
  who isn't sick. Lock the remaining sick people in a back room. Ignore the healthy crowd
  entirely. Now count: of these sick-only people, what share got a beep? The denominator
  shrank to the back-room.

  The link between them (true by definition, not a formula to memorise):

      P(A, B)  =  P(B | A) * P(A)
      (whole-crowd both)  =  (back-room share) * (whole-crowd share of A)

  Rearranged: P(B | A) = P(A, B) / P(A). In words: to go from the joint to the conditional,
  divide by the size of the back-room.

  The two questions these answer:
  - P(Sick, Beep): "Of everyone who walked past the stall today, what share was sick AND
    beeped?" Denominator = whole crowd.
  - P(Beep | Sick): "Of the sick people specifically, what share did the machine catch?"
    Denominator = sick people only.

  They measure different things. Confusing them is the single most common Bayes mistake.


  ## The Shopkeeper Hides P(Beep) -- So We Rebuild It by Gluing

  The doctor's question -- "given this beep, am I sick?" -- would be trivial if the greedy
  shopkeeper told us the total share of beep papers. He refuses. So we build that total
  ourselves from the only two ways a beep paper is born:

      WAY 1: a SICK person the machine caught.
             share = P(Beep | Sick) * P(Sick)

      WAY 2: a HEALTHY person the machine glitched on.
             share = P(Beep | Healthy) * P(Healthy)

  These two crowds never overlap (a person is either sick or healthy, never both). So we just
  add them:

      P(Beep) = P(Beep|Sick)*P(Sick)  +  P(Beep|Healthy)*P(Healthy)

  This is the Law of Total Probability -- the total beep count is the sum of beeps from
  separate, non-overlapping sub-crowds. Three sub-crowds (disease A, disease B, healthy C)?
  Glue three terms:

      P(Beep) = P(Beep|A)*P(A) + P(Beep|B)*P(B) + P(Beep|Healthy)*P(Healthy)

  Plug this gluing into P(A|Beep) = P(A, Beep) / P(Beep) and you get the full Bayes rule:

      P(Sick | Beep)  =       P(Beep | Sick) * P(Sick)
                         -----------------------------------------------
                         P(Beep|Sick)*P(Sick) + P(Beep|Healthy)*P(Healthy)

  You never need to memorise this formula. Just build the Sunday crowd, count the beepers by
  sub-crowd, glue them, and divide the relevant pile by the total.


  ## The Sick Who Walked Away

  The machine missed them. They hold a clean paper. Bayes never counted them -- the whole
  machinery above only ever looks at people whose paper beeped. The missed cases are outside
  the calculation, back in the wild, untreated.

  This is not a theoretical concern. In a disease with 1% prevalence (10 sick per 1,000
  people), a machine that catches 90% of the sick still sends 1 sick person per 1,000 back
  into the wild holding a clean paper. That 1 person does not know they are sick. For an
  infectious disease, that 1 matters far more than 99 false alarms. The Bayes number
  (P(Sick | Beep)) measures the doctor's precision on beepers. It says nothing at all about
  the danger of the silence.

  The lesson: for safety tests -- disease screening, structural fault detection -- cutting
  the silent misses (raising the machine's catch rate on the sick) usually matters far more
  than reducing false alarms. Bayes gives you precision. The wild gives you the other bill.


  ## Ten Problems, One Sunday Crowd

  The same move on every problem: make the fractions into heads, draw the two piles
  (sick and healthy), run each through the machine, count the beepers, divide.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 1 -- conditional from joint
  Given: P(Sick, Beep) = 0.15  and  P(Sick) = 0.50.   Find: P(Beep | Sick).

  100 souls this Sunday. 50 are sick (P(Sick)=0.50). Of the whole 100, 15 are sick AND
  beeped (P(Sick, Beep)=0.15). Lock the 50 sick in the back room. 15 of them beeped.

      P(Beep | Sick) = 15 / 50 = 0.30

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 2 -- joint from conditional
  Given: P(Sick) = 0.30  and  P(Beep | Sick) = 0.20.   Find: P(Sick, Beep).

  100 souls. 30 are sick. The machine catches only 20% of them: 30 * 0.20 = 6 sick-and-
  beeping. Out of the whole 100, that is:

      P(Sick, Beep) = 6 / 100 = 0.06

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 3 -- classic Bayes: the shopkeeper hands us P(Beep) directly
  Given: P(Sick) = 0.30, P(Beep | Sick) = 0.80, P(Beep) = 0.50.   Find: P(Sick | Beep).

  100 souls. 30 truly sick. Machine catches 80% of them: 30 * 0.80 = 24 true beeps. The
  shopkeeper tells us he handed out 50 beep papers total today. A patient clutches a beep --
  one of the 50. Truly sick?

      P(Sick | Beep) = 24 / 50 = 0.48

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 4 -- rare disease (1%): the rarity trap, first encounter
  Given: P(D) = 0.01, P(Beep | D) = 0.90, P(Beep | Healthy) = 0.10.
  Find: P(D | Beep).

  1,000 souls this Sunday.

      Sick (1% = 10): machine catches 90%  ->  9 true beeps.   1 missed -- walks into the wild.
      Healthy (990):  machine glitches 10% ->  99 false beeps.

  Glue the beep papers:  9 + 99 = 108 total.
  A patient holds a beep. Truly sick?

      P(D | Beep) = 9 / 108 = 0.0833   ->  about 8%.

  A beep means sick only 8% of the time. The disease is so rare (1%) that the machine's
  10% glitch rate on the huge healthy crowd (99 false beeps) swamps the tiny sick crowd
  (9 true beeps). This is the rarity trap: a positive test result on a rare disease is
  mostly noise. In the wild: 1 sick person per 1,000 walked away clean and undiagnosed.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 5 -- common disease (30%): when prevalence fights the glitch
  Given: P(Sick) = 0.30, P(Beep | Sick) = 0.60, P(Beep | Healthy) = 0.20.
  Find: P(Sick | Beep).

  100 souls.

      Sick (30): machine catches 60%  ->  18 true beeps.  12 missed -- into the wild.
      Healthy (70): machine glitches 20%  ->  14 false beeps.

  Glue:  18 + 14 = 32.

      P(Sick | Beep) = 18 / 32 = 0.5625   ->  about 56%.

  Now prevalence fights back: 30% sick is common enough that the 18 true beeps outnumber
  the 14 glitches. But 12 sick people walked away clean -- more than the 9 in Problem 4.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 6 -- rarity trap deepened (2%): the small sick crowd drowns again
  Given: P(D) = 0.02, P(Beep | D) = 0.80, P(Beep | Healthy) = 0.05.
  Find: P(D | Beep).

  1,000 souls.

      Sick (2% = 20): caught 80%  ->  16 true beeps.   4 missed -- into the wild.
      Healthy (980):  glitch 5%   ->  49 false beeps.

  Glue:  16 + 49 = 65.

      P(D | Beep) = 16 / 65 = 0.2462   ->  about 25%.

  The machine is better (80% catch rate, only 5% glitch rate) than in Problem 4. Yet a beep
  is still only right one time in four. Why? The disease is rare (2%), so the tiny sick crowd
  (16) is drowned by even a small glitch rate on the huge healthy crowd (49). The sting of
  the rarity trap does not go away just because the machine is better. It goes away when
  prevalence rises.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 7 -- two causes: Disease AND a harmless Sniffle confuse the machine
  Given: P(D) = 0.01, P(Sniffle) = 0.05 (independent of D).
  Machine rates:   P(Beep | D, Sniffle) = 0.99
                   P(Beep | D, no Sniffle) = 0.95
                   P(Beep | no D, Sniffle) = 0.90
                   P(Beep | no D, no Sniffle) = 0.02  (pure glitch)
  Find: P(D | Beep).

  This Sunday, 10,000 souls pass the stall. Four corners of the crowd:

      D & Sniffle:    0.01 * 0.05 * 10,000 =    5   ->  beep 0.99  ->    4.95
      D only:         0.01 * 0.95 * 10,000 =   95   ->  beep 0.95  ->   90.25
      Sniffle only:   0.99 * 0.05 * 10,000 =  495   ->  beep 0.90  ->  445.50
      Neither:        0.99 * 0.95 * 10,000 = 9405   ->  beep 0.02  ->  188.10

  Glue all beep papers:  4.95 + 90.25 + 445.50 + 188.10 = 728.80.
  Of those, the ones who truly have the Disease = 4.95 + 90.25 = 95.20.

      P(D | Beep) = 95.20 / 728.80 = 0.1306   ->  about 13%.

  The harmless Sniffle (common: 5%) triggers the machine at 90%, flooding the pile with
  445 sniffle-beeps. The glitch-on-neither adds 188 more. Real Disease beeps (95) are lost
  in the noise. A beep means the actual Disease only 13% of the time.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 8 -- two machines (the shopkeeper charges you twice)
  Given: P(H) = 0.10. Two independent machines.
  Machine 1: P(M1 | H) = 0.80, P(M1 | Healthy) = 0.20.
  Machine 2: P(M2 | H) = 0.70, P(M2 | Healthy) = 0.10.
  Doctor only acts when BOTH papers beep.   Find: P(H | M1, M2).

  1,000 souls.

      Sick (10% = 100):
          M1 beeps 80% -> 80.   Of those, M2 beeps 70% -> 56 get both papers.
          44 sick get fewer than both -- sent home -- into the wild.
      Healthy (900):
          M1 glitches 20% -> 180.   Of those, M2 glitches 10% -> 18 get both papers.

  Glue the double-beepers:  56 + 18 = 74.

      P(H | M1, M2) = 56 / 74 = 0.7568   ->  about 76%.

  Two machines in series raise precision dramatically: from a single-machine ~44% (try it:
  same H=10%, catch=80%, glitch=20%) to 76%. The price: 44 sick people failed both tests and
  walked into the wild. More machines to confirm = fewer false alarms = more missed cases.
  There is no free lunch.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 9 -- the useless scribble (ignore the distraction)
  Given: P(D) = 0.30, P(Beep | D) = 0.80, P(Beep | Healthy) = 0.20.
  The shopkeeper also scrawls a red-coat note on every paper ("patient wore a red coat") --
  a trap clue the doctor must ignore. Find: P(D | Beep).

  100 souls.

      Sick (30): machine beeps 80%  ->  24 true beeps.   6 missed -- into the wild.
      Healthy (70): machine glitches 20%  ->  14 false beeps.

  Glue:  24 + 14 = 38.

      P(D | Beep) = 24 / 38 = 0.6316   ->  about 63%.

  The red-coat note is irrelevant. It does not appear in the calculation. The trap is
  including it -- spending mental effort on a variable that has no power to change the
  answer. The Sunday crowd ignores it automatically: you counted beepers, not coat colours.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  PROBLEM 10 -- three problems in one number
  Turn Problems 4, 6, 9 into one table to see what actually moves the needle:

      Problem   P(D)   catch rate   glitch rate   P(D|Beep)   missed per 1,000
      -------   ----   ----------   -----------   ---------   ----------------
        P4      1%        90%          10%           8.3%           1
        P6      2%        80%           5%          24.6%           4
        P9     30%        80%          20%          63.2%          60

  The rarity trap is real: going from 1% to 2% prevalence (doubling the sick share) with a
  better machine raises the positive-test precision from 8% to 25%. Going to 30% prevalence
  pushes it to 63%. Prevalence is the dominant lever. A beep from a rare-disease machine is
  almost always a glitch -- even with a 90% catch rate. Meanwhile the absolute missed count
  grows with prevalence: at 30% sick, 60 per 1,000 walk into the wild untreated. Precision
  and recall pull opposite directions. The machine can only trade between them.


  ## Python: Three Cases Hard-Coded, Then a Full Simulation

  Three problems shown variable by variable -- no loops, every number on a line -- then the
  full Monte Carlo simulation (100,000 souls per run) to verify all ten answers.

  ```python
  # -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  # PART 1: PROBLEMS 4, 5, 6 -- hard-coded, line by line, no loops
  # Crowd size 1,000 for P4/P6; 100 for P5. Every number explicit.
  # -----------------------------------------------------------------------

  # ---- PROBLEM 4 (rare disease: 1%, catch 90%, glitch 10%, 1000 souls) ----
  crowd_p4         = 1000
  sick_p4          = crowd_p4 * 0.01           # 10  (truly sick)
  healthy_p4       = crowd_p4 - sick_p4        # 990

  true_beeps_p4    = sick_p4 * 0.90            # 9   (sick and caught)
  missed_p4        = sick_p4 * 0.10            # 1   (sick and missed -> wild)
  false_beeps_p4   = healthy_p4 * 0.10         # 99  (healthy and glitched)

  total_beeps_p4   = true_beeps_p4 + false_beeps_p4   # 108
  p_sick_given_beep_p4 = true_beeps_p4 / total_beeps_p4

  print(f"P4: true={true_beeps_p4:.0f}, glitch={false_beeps_p4:.0f}, "
        f"total={total_beeps_p4:.0f}, P(D|Beep)={p_sick_given_beep_p4:.4f}")
  # P4: true=9, glitch=99, total=108, P(D|Beep)=0.0833
  print(f"P4: missed into wild = {missed_p4:.0f} per {crowd_p4} people")
  # P4: missed into wild = 1 per 1000 people


  # ---- PROBLEM 5 (30% flu: catch 60%, glitch 20%, 100 souls) ----
  crowd_p5         = 100
  sick_p5          = crowd_p5 * 0.30           # 30
  healthy_p5       = crowd_p5 - sick_p5        # 70

  true_beeps_p5    = sick_p5 * 0.60            # 18
  missed_p5        = sick_p5 * 0.40            # 12  (into wild)
  false_beeps_p5   = healthy_p5 * 0.20         # 14

  total_beeps_p5   = true_beeps_p5 + false_beeps_p5   # 32
  p_sick_given_beep_p5 = true_beeps_p5 / total_beeps_p5

  print(f"P5: true={true_beeps_p5:.0f}, glitch={false_beeps_p5:.0f}, "
        f"total={total_beeps_p5:.0f}, P(Sick|Beep)={p_sick_given_beep_p5:.4f}")
  # P5: true=18, glitch=14, total=32, P(Sick|Beep)=0.5625
  print(f"P5: missed into wild = {missed_p5:.0f} per {crowd_p5} people")
  # P5: missed into wild = 12 per 100 people


  # ---- PROBLEM 6 (2% disease: catch 80%, glitch 5%, 1000 souls) ----
  crowd_p6         = 1000
  sick_p6          = crowd_p6 * 0.02           # 20
  healthy_p6       = crowd_p6 - sick_p6        # 980

  true_beeps_p6    = sick_p6 * 0.80            # 16
  missed_p6        = sick_p6 * 0.20            # 4   (into wild)
  false_beeps_p6   = healthy_p6 * 0.05         # 49

  total_beeps_p6   = true_beeps_p6 + false_beeps_p6   # 65
  p_sick_given_beep_p6 = true_beeps_p6 / total_beeps_p6

  print(f"P6: true={true_beeps_p6:.0f}, glitch={false_beeps_p6:.0f}, "
        f"total={total_beeps_p6:.0f}, P(D|Beep)={p_sick_given_beep_p6:.4f}")
  # P6: true=16, glitch=49, total=65, P(D|Beep)=0.2462
  print(f"P6: missed into wild = {missed_p6:.0f} per {crowd_p6} people")
  # P6: missed into wild = 4 per 1000 people

  # The rarity trap visible in one comparison:
  print(f"\nRarity trap: P4 precision={p_sick_given_beep_p4:.2%}, "
        f"P6 precision={p_sick_given_beep_p6:.2%}")
  # Rarity trap: P4 precision=8.33%, P6 precision=24.62%
  # Doubling prevalence (1%->2%) with a better machine triples precision.
  # Prevalence is the dominant lever, not the machine.
  ```

  Output of Part 1:

      P4: true=9, glitch=99, total=108, P(D|Beep)=0.0833
      P4: missed into wild = 1 per 1000 people
      P5: true=18, glitch=14, total=32, P(Sick|Beep)=0.5625
      P5: missed into wild = 12 per 100 people
      P6: true=16, glitch=49, total=65, P(D|Beep)=0.2462
      P6: missed into wild = 4 per 1000 people
      Rarity trap: P4 precision=8.33%, P6 precision=24.62%

  ```python
  # -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  # PART 2: FULL SIMULATION -- 100,000 souls, every problem verified
  # Reskinned to match the shopkeeper-and-doctor world exactly.
  # One loop per problem so the numbers stay visible; no abstraction.
  # (A loop here hides the per-person coin flip, not the Bayes logic --
  #  100,000 explicit if-statements would make the same point more slowly.)
  # -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  import random

  def run_simulation(n=100_000):
      print(f"Running {n:,}-soul simulation (shopkeeper's world)...")
      print("=" * 60)

      # ---- PROBLEM 4: rare disease 1%, catch 90%, glitch 10% ----
      p4_true = 0;  p4_glitch = 0;  p4_missed = 0
      for _ in range(n):
          sick = random.random() < 0.01
          if sick:
              if random.random() < 0.90: p4_true   += 1
              else:                      p4_missed  += 1
          else:
              if random.random() < 0.10: p4_glitch  += 1
      total = p4_true + p4_glitch
      print(f"P4 (rare disease 1%):  total beeps={total}, "
            f"true={p4_true}, glitch={p4_glitch}, missed->wild={p4_missed}")
      print(f"  Empirical P(D|Beep)={p4_true/total:.4f}   Theory: 0.0833")
      print("-" * 60)

      # ---- PROBLEM 5: 30% flu, catch 60%, glitch 20% ----
      p5_true = 0;  p5_glitch = 0;  p5_missed = 0
      for _ in range(n):
          sick = random.random() < 0.30
          if sick:
              if random.random() < 0.60: p5_true   += 1
              else:                      p5_missed  += 1
          else:
              if random.random() < 0.20: p5_glitch  += 1
      total = p5_true + p5_glitch
      print(f"P5 (30% flu):          total beeps={total}, "
            f"true={p5_true}, glitch={p5_glitch}, missed->wild={p5_missed}")
      print(f"  Empirical P(Sick|Beep)={p5_true/total:.4f}   Theory: 0.5625")
      print("-" * 60)

      # ---- PROBLEM 6: 2% disease, catch 80%, glitch 5% ----
      p6_true = 0;  p6_glitch = 0;  p6_missed = 0
      for _ in range(n):
          sick = random.random() < 0.02
          if sick:
              if random.random() < 0.80: p6_true   += 1
              else:                      p6_missed  += 1
          else:
              if random.random() < 0.05: p6_glitch  += 1
      total = p6_true + p6_glitch
      print(f"P6 (2% disease):       total beeps={total}, "
            f"true={p6_true}, glitch={p6_glitch}, missed->wild={p6_missed}")
      print(f"  Empirical P(D|Beep)={p6_true/total:.4f}   Theory: 0.2462")
      print("-" * 60)

      # ---- PROBLEM 7: Disease(1%) + Sniffle(5%), four-corner machine ----
      # The machine beeps at a different rate depending on which a person carries.
      # Disease and Sniffle are independent -- a person can have both, either, or neither.
      p7_beep_and_disease = 0;  p7_beep_no_disease = 0;  p7_disease_missed = 0
      for _ in range(n):
          has_disease = random.random() < 0.01
          has_sniffle = random.random() < 0.05
          if   has_disease and     has_sniffle:  beep_rate = 0.99
          elif has_disease and not has_sniffle:  beep_rate = 0.95
          elif not has_disease and has_sniffle:  beep_rate = 0.90
          else:                                  beep_rate = 0.02   # pure glitch
          beeped = random.random() < beep_rate
          if beeped:
              if has_disease: p7_beep_and_disease += 1
              else:           p7_beep_no_disease  += 1
          else:
              if has_disease: p7_disease_missed   += 1
      total = p7_beep_and_disease + p7_beep_no_disease
      print(f"P7 (Disease+Sniffle):  total beeps={total}, "
            f"disease_beeps={p7_beep_and_disease}, other={p7_beep_no_disease}, "
            f"missed->wild={p7_disease_missed}")
      print(f"  Empirical P(D|Beep)={p7_beep_and_disease/total:.4f}   Theory: 0.1306")
      print("-" * 60)

      # ---- PROBLEM 8: two machines in series, both must beep ----
      # Machine 1: catch=80%/glitch=20%.  Machine 2: catch=70%/glitch=10%.
      p8_both_sick = 0;  p8_both_healthy = 0;  p8_sick_missed = 0
      for _ in range(n):
          sick = random.random() < 0.10
          if sick:
              m1 = random.random() < 0.80
              m2 = random.random() < 0.70
              if m1 and m2:  p8_both_sick    += 1
              else:          p8_sick_missed  += 1
          else:
              m1 = random.random() < 0.20
              m2 = random.random() < 0.10
              if m1 and m2:  p8_both_healthy += 1
      total = p8_both_sick + p8_both_healthy
      print(f"P8 (two machines):     double beeps={total}, "
            f"sick={p8_both_sick}, healthy={p8_both_healthy}, "
            f"sick missed->wild={p8_sick_missed}")
      print(f"  Empirical P(H|M1,M2)={p8_both_sick/total:.4f}   Theory: 0.7568")
      print("-" * 60)

      # ---- PROBLEM 9: useless red-coat scribble -- ignore it ----
      # Disease 30%, machine 80%/20%.  Red-coat note is noise; we never use it.
      p9_true = 0;  p9_glitch = 0;  p9_missed = 0
      for _ in range(n):
          sick = random.random() < 0.30
          _red_coat = random.random() < 0.50    # shopkeeper's scribble -- irrelevant
          if sick:
              if random.random() < 0.80: p9_true   += 1
              else:                      p9_missed  += 1
          else:
              if random.random() < 0.20: p9_glitch  += 1
      total = p9_true + p9_glitch
      print(f"P9 (useless scribble): total beeps={total}, "
            f"true={p9_true}, glitch={p9_glitch}, missed->wild={p9_missed}")
      print(f"  Empirical P(D|Beep)={p9_true/total:.4f}   Theory: 0.6316")
      print(f"  (Red-coat note was generated but never used -- _red_coat discarded)")
      print("=" * 60)

  run_simulation()
  ```

  ## The Formula (it was here all along)

  You have now used Bayes' rule ten times without reading it as a formula. Here it is, in case
  the quiz asks:

      P(Sick | Beep)  =       P(Beep | Sick) * P(Sick)
                         -----------------------------------------------
                         P(Beep|Sick)*P(Sick) + P(Beep|Healthy)*P(Healthy)

  Every term in it is a pile of heads you already counted:
  - P(Beep | Sick): of the back-room of sick people, what share beeped?
  - P(Sick): what share of the whole Sunday crowd is sick?
  - The denominator: the total beep papers glued from the two piles.
  - P(Sick | Beep): of all the beep papers, what share went to sick people?

  The formula is not magic. It is the Sunday crowd count, compressed into one line.


  ## The Decoder (jargon -> plain, master sheet)

      PEACOCK WORD                       PLAIN WORD (shopkeeper's world)
      --------------------------------   -----------------------------------------------
      prior probability P(H)             share of the Sunday crowd that is sick before
                                         the machine sees them
      likelihood P(Beep | H)            of the sick in the back-room, what share beeps
      marginal / evidence P(Beep)        total beep papers -- the number the shopkeeper
                                         hides; we glue it from the sub-crowds
      posterior probability P(H | Beep) of the beepers, what share is truly sick --
                                         the doctor's question answered
      false positive / false alarm        healthy person who got a BEEP paper (a glitch)
      false negative / miss              sick person the machine stayed silent on -- walks
                                         into the wild undiagnosed
      sensitivity / recall / TPR        share of the sick the machine caught (true beeps
                                         divided by all sick)
      specificity / TNR                  share of the healthy the machine stayed silent on
                                         (true silences divided by all healthy)
      precision / PPV                    share of beepers who are truly sick --
                                         P(Sick | Beep); the doctor's number
      Law of Total Probability           the gluing move: P(Beep) = sum of P(Beep|group)*P(group)
                                         over all non-overlapping sub-crowds
      Bayes' rule / Bayes' theorem       divide the sub-crowd you want by the total beepers


  ---

  One Breath: make every fraction into heads in the Sunday crowd; a BEEP paper is born two
  ways (sick person caught, or healthy person glitched), so glue those two piles to find the
  total; divide the sick-and-beeped pile by the total and you have the doctor's answer --
  and the rarity trap is just the arithmetic fact that a tiny sick crowd, even well-caught,
  is drowned by even a small glitch rate on the vast healthy crowd; every person the machine
  stayed silent on is already walking in the wild.
